A sump pump failure in Apex usually gets noticed at the worst possible moment: in the middle of a heavy rain event, when the basement or crawlspace it's supposed to protect is exactly the space filling with water. Wake County's clay-heavy soil holds water at the surface longer than sandier soil, which puts real pressure on basement waterproofing systems during storms.

Some homes never had a sump pump installed at all, particularly older properties, and add one only after a first flooding scare. Both situations, a failed existing pump and a first-time install, get handled with the same priority given the timing risk involved.

Why Sump Pumps Fail

A stuck or jammed float switch is the most common failure, since the switch physically triggers the pump and can get lodged by debris in the pit. Power loss during a storm is the second most common, since a sump pump running on standard household power stops the moment the power does, exactly when it's needed most.

A pump that's simply aged out, most units last 7 to 10 years, will also start failing intermittently before it stops completely, which is why a pump making unusual noise is worth checking before the next storm rather than after a flood.

Signs a Sump Pump Needs Attention

  • The pump doesn't turn on when water reaches the float switch
  • Unusual grinding, rattling, or humming from the pump
  • The pump runs constantly, even without recent rain
  • Visible rust or corrosion on the unit
  • The pump is 7+ years old and has never been serviced

What Gets Checked

The float switch and check valve are tested directly, since a stuck switch or a failed check valve (which lets pumped water flow back into the pit) are the two most common repairable issues. The discharge line is also checked to confirm water is actually being carried away from the foundation rather than pooling back nearby.

For a home without battery backup, that gap gets flagged directly. A battery or water-powered backup pump keeps working through a power outage, which is precisely when a primary pump is most likely to fail.

Repair vs. Replace vs. First Install

Stuck float or bad check valve

Repairable in most cases, often same-visit.

Pump motor failed or 8+ years old

Replacement is usually more cost-effective than repeated repairs.

No backup power source

Adding a battery or water-powered backup pump alongside the primary unit.

No sump pump installed at all

A full new installation, sized to the specific basement or crawlspace.

Basement Flooding During a Storm?

Before Help Arrives

  • Switch off electrical outlets or equipment in the affected area if it's safe to reach.
  • Move stored items and valuables away from the flooding path.
  • Avoid walking through standing water near any electrical source.
  • Photograph the flooding before cleanup, for insurance purposes.

Cost and Coverage

A straightforward float or valve repair costs meaningfully less than a full pump replacement, which is why diagnosing the actual failure point matters before assuming the whole unit needs to go. Adding battery backup to an existing pump is typically less than a full new installation.

Homeowners insurance treatment of sump pump failure and resulting water damage varies significantly by policy. Some require a specific sump-pump-failure endorsement, so it's worth checking before assuming standard coverage applies.

A generator or battery backup system already in the home for other reasons sometimes covers the sump pump too, worth mentioning during the visit so the setup isn't duplicated unnecessarily.
