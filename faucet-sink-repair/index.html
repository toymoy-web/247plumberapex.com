Faucet & Sink Repair in Apex, NC

A dripping faucet, a stuck handle, or a leak under the sink all get diagnosed on site before deciding on a repair or a replacement part.

A dripping faucet is more than an annoyance. Left long enough, a worn cartridge or washer can waste a meaningful amount of water and drive up a bill without anyone noticing the cause. In Apex, NC, faucet and sink repair calls range from a simple washer replacement to reworking the supply and drain connections under an older sink.

Fixture failures cluster around a few common parts: the cartridge or valve inside the faucet body, the supply line connections beneath the sink, and the sink drain assembly itself. Identifying which one is actually failing before quoting a fix keeps the repair focused rather than replacing more than necessary.

Why Faucets and Sinks Fail in Apex Homes

Mineral buildup from Apex's harder municipal water accelerates wear inside a faucet cartridge or valve, which is why a fixture in an older home often needs attention sooner than the same fixture would in an area with softer water.

Supply line connections under a sink, especially older compression fittings rather than modern quarter-turn shutoffs, are also a common source of slow leaks that go unnoticed until a cabinet floor shows water damage.

Signs It's Time for a Repair

  • A faucet that drips even when fully shut off
  • A handle that's stiff, loose, or hard to turn
  • Water pooling under the sink or inside the cabinet
  • Low pressure at one specific faucet, not the whole house
  • Visible corrosion on supply lines or shutoff valves

Diagnosing the Actual Failure Point

A dripping faucet almost always traces back to a worn cartridge, valve seat, or O-ring inside the fixture body, checked directly rather than replacing the whole faucet on a guess. A leak under the sink gets traced to its specific connection point, a supply line, a shutoff valve, or the drain trap, before anything gets tightened or replaced.

Low pressure isolated to a single fixture often points to a clogged aerator or a partially closed shutoff valve rather than a whole-house pressure problem, which is a much simpler and less expensive fix once correctly identified.

Repair vs. Replacement

Worn cartridge or valve

Direct replacement of the internal part, usually same-visit.

Corroded or failing supply line

Line and shutoff valve replacement, quarter-turn valves recommended over old compression fittings.

Fixture body cracked or beyond repair

Full faucet replacement, matching or upgrading the existing finish and style.

Sink drain assembly leaking

Trap and drain assembly repair or replacement as needed.

What to Do Before We Arrive

  • Shut off the fixture's individual shutoff valve if a leak is active and accessible.
  • Place a towel or container under an active drip to limit water on the cabinet floor.
  • Avoid using the affected fixture until it's been checked if the leak is significant.

Cost and What's Included

A cartridge or valve replacement is one of the more affordable repairs in residential plumbing, and most common parts are stocked and completed in a single visit. A full faucet replacement costs more depending on the fixture chosen but is usually straightforward once the old unit is removed.

Supply line and shutoff valve replacement is often bundled with a faucet repair when the connections underneath show wear, since replacing an aging valve at the same time avoids a second service call down the road.

Kitchen faucets tend to fail sooner than bathroom faucets given the heavier daily use, which is worth factoring in when deciding whether a repair or a full replacement makes more sense for a high-traffic fixture. Any supply line or valve work follows current Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requirements as adopted under North Carolina's plumbing code.
