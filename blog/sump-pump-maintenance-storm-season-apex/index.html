Sump Pump Maintenance Before Piedmont Storm Season

By the Apex 24/7 Plumbing team · 2026-09-15

Have this exact problem right now? Skip the reading and call (919) 944-7281 directly.

A sump pump only matters in the moment it's actually needed, which is exactly why it's so easy to forget about until a heavy storm reveals whether it's still working. In Apex and the surrounding Piedmont region, that moment tends to arrive with little warning, a fast-moving storm dropping more rain than the ground can absorb quickly.

Wake County's clay-heavy soil is a real factor here. Clay holds water at the surface far longer than sandier soil, which means more sustained pressure against a home's foundation and basement waterproofing system during and after a significant rain event.

Testing a Pump in Five Minutes

The simplest test: pour a bucket of water into the sump pit until the float switch triggers. The pump should activate immediately, move the water out, and shut off cleanly once the level drops. Any hesitation, unusual noise, or failure to trigger at all is worth investigating before storm season arrives, not during it.

The Float Switch Is the Most Common Failure Point

A stuck or jammed float switch is the single most common reason a sump pump fails to activate when it's needed. Debris in the pit, a misaligned float, or simple age can all cause the switch to stop triggering reliably. This is also the easiest component to check and often the easiest to fix.

What Happens When the Power Goes Out

A standard sump pump runs on household electricity, which means it stops working the instant the power does. Since major storms are exactly when power outages are most likely, a primary pump with no backup power source is protecting a home only up until the moment it's needed most.

A battery backup or water-powered backup pump keeps the system running through an outage, which closes exactly the gap that matters most during a real storm event.

What to Do Before the Next Storm

  • Test the float switch with a bucket of water
  • Check the discharge line for clogs or blockages outside the house
  • Confirm the pump isn't more than 7-10 years old without a checkup
  • Verify a battery or water-powered backup is in place
  • Clear any debris from around the sump pit

Signs a Pump Needs Professional Attention

Grinding or rattling sounds, a pump that runs constantly even without recent rain, or visible rust and corrosion on the unit are all signs worth addressing before the next storm rather than waiting to see if the pump makes it through one more event.

Where Discharge Lines Go Wrong

A pump can be working perfectly and still fail to protect a basement if the discharge line, the pipe carrying pumped water away from the house, is clogged, frozen, or simply routed too close to the foundation. Water discharged right back toward the house defeats much of the pump's purpose, so the discharge point deserves the same attention as the pump itself.

Checking that the discharge line's outdoor exit is clear of debris and directs water well away from the foundation is a simple step that's easy to overlook during a basic pump test.

New Installation vs. Aging Pump

Some Apex homes, particularly older properties, never had a sump pump installed at all until a first flooding scare prompted one. If that's the situation, a new installation gets sized to the specific basement or crawlspace rather than installed as a generic, one-size-fits-all fix.

For homes with an existing pump approaching or past the typical 7 to 10 year service life, a proactive checkup before storm season is far less stressful than discovering a failure during an actual flood.

If a pump fails the bucket test, makes unusual noise, or doesn't have backup power in place, a sump pump repair or installation visit checks the float switch, check valve, and discharge line before recommending a fix, and if flooding has already caused damage, restoration work addresses that directly. This comes up often in Beaver Creek, where several neighborhoods sit close to the Beaver Creek Greenway's low-lying, slower-draining ground.

How Often Should a Pump Actually Be Tested?

A simple bucket test is worth doing at least twice a year for most Apex homes, once before the region's typical spring storm pattern picks up and once in early fall before the wetter months settle in. A home with a history of basement moisture or a pump that's already showing its age benefits from checking more frequently than that.

This is a five-minute task that costs nothing beyond a bucket of water, which makes it one of the easiest pieces of home maintenance to actually follow through on compared to more involved seasonal chores.

Battery Backup Isn't a Luxury Item

It's worth restating plainly: a sump pump with no backup power source is protecting a home right up until the exact moment it's needed most, since major storms are precisely when power outages happen. A battery or water-powered backup closes that gap directly and is a relatively small addition compared to the cost of basement water damage.

For homes that already have a whole-house generator for other reasons, confirming the sump pump circuit is actually covered by it is worth checking directly rather than assuming it is.

Secondary Pumps and Multiple-Pump Systems

Some Apex homes with a history of serious basement moisture use a secondary backup pump in addition to the primary unit, positioned slightly higher in the pit so it only activates if the primary pump can't keep up with an unusually heavy inflow. This is worth considering for a basement with finished living space or stored valuables where even a short pump failure could cause meaningful damage.

A secondary pump adds cost and a bit of complexity, but for the right home, the additional protection is a reasonable tradeoff against the potential cost of a flooded finished basement.

What a Professional Checkup Actually Covers

Beyond the basic bucket test a homeowner can do themselves, a professional checkup verifies the check valve is preventing backflow into the pit, confirms the discharge line has no hidden clogs along its full run, and checks the pump's overall condition for signs of wear that a simple activation test won't reveal on its own.

This deeper check is worth doing at least once every couple of years, even for a pump that's passing basic bucket tests reliably, since some failure modes only show up under sustained real-world conditions rather than a brief test.

None of this needs to be complicated. A pump that's tested regularly, has working backup power, and gets a professional look every couple of years is about as prepared as any home can reasonably be for whatever the next storm brings.

Whether it's a first-time question about installing a pump or a routine checkup on an existing one, treating this as regular seasonal maintenance rather than an emergency reaction after a flood is by far the better position to be in.
