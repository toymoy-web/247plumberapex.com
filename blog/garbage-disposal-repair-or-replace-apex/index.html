How to Tell If Your Garbage Disposal Needs Repair or Replacement

By the Apex 24/7 Plumbing team · 2026-09-15

Have this exact problem right now? Skip the reading and call (919) 944-7281 directly.

A garbage disposal problem tends to trigger an immediate assumption: it's broken, time for a new one. In practice, a meaningful share of disposal calls turn out to be a simple mechanical jam rather than an actual failure, which is worth knowing before assuming a full replacement is the only option.

The Humming Sound Almost Always Means a Jam

If the disposal hums when switched on but the blades don't spin, that's the motor trying to turn against something physically blocking it, not a dead motor. Most disposals include a manual reset feature, either a small hex-key hole on the bottom of the unit or a reset button, specifically for clearing this kind of jam.

This single distinction, humming versus complete silence, is the fastest way to separate a simple fix from an actual failure.

Complete Silence Points Elsewhere

A disposal that makes no sound at all when switched on, no humming, nothing, is more likely an electrical issue: a tripped breaker, a blown fuse, or in some units, an internal overload switch that's tripped and needs resetting. This isn't the same problem as a jam and doesn't respond to the same fixes.

When a Leak Means Repair vs. Replace

Where a disposal is leaking matters more than the fact that it's leaking at all. A leak at the sink flange connection or the dishwasher drain line hookup is a serviceable seal issue, repairable without replacing the whole unit. A leak from the disposal's body itself, the housing where the grinding mechanism lives, generally isn't repairable and does mean replacement.

Age as a Factor, Not the Whole Story

Garbage disposals typically last 8 to 12 years with normal use. A unit within that range having its first real issue is usually worth repairing. A unit at or past that age showing multiple symptoms at once is a more reasonable candidate for proactive replacement, especially if repeated jams suggest the motor itself is weakening rather than a single object simply getting stuck.

A Simple Decision Framework

  • Humming, no spin: try the manual reset first
  • Complete silence: check the breaker and reset button before assuming failure
  • Leaking at a connection point: repairable seal issue
  • Leaking from the unit body: replacement needed
  • Burning smell or repeated failures on an 8+ year old unit: replacement worth considering

What Not to Do

Never reach into a disposal by hand to clear a jam, even when it's switched off, since these units can sometimes be accidentally reactivated. Always use the manual reset tool or a long-handled tool like tongs, and switch off power at the wall or breaker for anything beyond a quick manual reset attempt.

What Actually Causes Most Jams

Fibrous foods like celery, corn husks, and onion skins are among the most common jam culprits, since their stringy texture wraps around the spinning components rather than grinding cleanly. Bones, fruit pits, and anything genuinely hard can also jam or, worse, chip the grinding components over time even if they don't cause an immediate stoppage.

In Apex's older, established neighborhoods like Haddon Hall and Beaver Creek, disposals original to homes from the 1990s and 2000s see more of these jams simply from age, as mounting hardware and seals wear out around the same time a kitchen sees its first real plumbing issues.

Grease and fat poured down the drain don't jam a disposal directly, but they solidify further down the line and contribute to slow drains and clogs that get blamed on the disposal when the actual problem is downstream.

How Long Should a Disposal Actually Last?

A quality disposal used normally, without regular abuse from inappropriate items, should reasonably reach 10 to 12 years of service. Heavy daily use in a large household, or a history of grinding items that shouldn't go down a disposal at all, can shorten that meaningfully.

Apex's harder municipal water through Town of Apex Water Resources also plays a role here, since mineral buildup on internal components can shorten a disposal's working life compared to a home running on softer water.

If a disposal is failing well before that range with no clear abuse history, it's worth asking whether the original installation used a unit sized appropriately for the household's actual usage in the first place.

Simple Habits That Extend Disposal Life

  • Run cold water during and briefly after grinding, not hot
  • Grind a few ice cubes occasionally to help clean the grinding components
  • Avoid fibrous, starchy, or hard items entirely rather than testing limits
  • Never use chemical drain cleaners in a disposal, which can damage internal seals

When Replacement Actually Makes More Sense Than Repair

Beyond the body-leak and age factors already covered, a disposal that's jammed repeatedly within a short period, even after clearing each jam successfully, may be developing a deeper motor or bearing issue that manual resets only temporarily mask. Repeated jamming on the same unit within weeks is worth a real diagnosis rather than treating each occurrence as an isolated, unrelated event.

A Note on Older vs. Newer Disposal Models

Older disposal models generally have less powerful motors and simpler grinding mechanisms than current units, which means they jam more easily and handle a narrower range of food waste comfortably. Replacing a chronically jam-prone older unit with a modern, higher-horsepower model sometimes solves a recurring problem more effectively than repeatedly repairing an underpowered original installation.

This is worth raising directly if a disposal seems to jam far more often than seems reasonable for normal household use, since the unit itself, not household habits, may be the actual limiting factor.

A brief conversation about typical household cooking habits and how often the disposal sees use helps determine whether an upgrade to a more powerful model makes sense as part of any replacement decision.

The Dishwasher Connection Point Worth Checking Too

Most disposals also connect to the dishwasher drain line, and a leak or clog at this connection sometimes gets mistaken for a disposal problem when the actual issue is at that junction specifically. If a leak appears only when the dishwasher drains, rather than during normal disposal use, that connection point is the more likely culprit.

This distinction matters for diagnosis, since fixing the wrong connection point wastes time and money without resolving the actual leak in the first place.

A technician checking a disposal complaint typically inspects both the disposal itself and this dishwasher connection together, since the two are close enough physically that addressing one without checking the other risks missing the real source of a reported leak, especially in kitchens where space under the sink is tight and access is limited enough that a quick visual check alone can miss the actual source.

This combined check adds only a few minutes to a standard visit but avoids the frustration of a second call for what turns out to be the same underlying leak reappearing after an incomplete first repair.

If a jam, leak, or dead unit doesn't resolve with the basics above, a garbage disposal repair visit diagnoses the specific issue before recommending a fix, and where a full replacement genuinely makes sense, that's handled the same visit, sized to the household's actual usage. A related kitchen sink issue sometimes surfaces during the same diagnosis, since the two systems share the same connection point under the sink.
