Signs of a Slab Leak Every Wake County Homeowner Should Know

By the Apex 24/7 Plumbing team · 2026-09-15

Have this exact problem right now? Skip the reading and call (919) 944-7281 directly.

A slab leak happens when a water line running underneath a home's concrete foundation develops a leak. It's one of the more misunderstood plumbing problems, mostly because people assume finding one means jackhammering the entire floor. In reality, most slab leaks are found and fixed through a small, targeted access point.

The bigger challenge is actually noticing one in the first place, since a slab leak doesn't announce itself the way a burst pipe does. It shows up as a handful of indirect symptoms that are easy to dismiss individually.

The Warm Spot That Isn't Normal

A hot water line leaking under a slab often creates a detectable warm spot on the floor directly above it. If one section of an otherwise cool concrete or tile floor feels noticeably warmer than the rest, especially in a spot with no obvious reason to be warm, that's worth paying attention to.

The Sound of Water With Everything Off

Turn off every fixture in the house and listen near the water meter or in a quiet room. A hissing or running-water sound with nothing actually turned on is one of the clearer signs that water is escaping somewhere in the system, and a slab leak is one of the more common culprits when the sound seems to come from the floor itself.

Why Wake County's Soil Matters Here

Wake County's clay-heavy soil expands and contracts with moisture more than sandier soil does. That movement puts steady, uneven pressure on pipes running underneath a slab, which is part of why slab leaks show up here even in homes that were built with reasonable-quality materials at the time.

Copper hot water lines are particularly susceptible, since decades of heat cycling accelerates internal corrosion faster than it does in a cold line carrying the same water. Any repair or reroute follows current Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requirements as adopted under North Carolina's plumbing code.

Cold Line Leaks vs. Hot Line Leaks

Most slab leak discussions focus on hot water lines, and for good reason, since they fail more often. But a cold line running under a slab can leak too, and it presents differently: no warm spot to notice, since the line isn't carrying heated water. A cold line slab leak is more likely to be caught through the sound test or the water bill increase than through any floor temperature change.

This is part of why relying on just one detection method, checking for a warm spot alone, for example, can miss a real problem. Multiple signs, checked together, give a much more reliable picture than any single symptom on its own.

Why Homeowners Fear the Wrong Thing

The most common misconception about slab leaks is that finding one automatically means major floor demolition. In practice, detection equipment, acoustic listening devices and thermal imaging, narrows the leak's location down to a specific, often quite small area before anyone touches the concrete. The actual access point is typically limited to that section.

In some cases, rerouting the affected line above the slab or through a wall cavity avoids slab access entirely, replacing the problematic run with new pipe that bypasses the concrete altogether. Which approach makes more sense depends on the specific leak's location and how the home's plumbing was originally laid out.

What Insurance Does and Doesn't Cover

Homeowners insurance often covers the resulting damage from a slab leak, water-damaged flooring or drywall, for example, but typically does not cover the cost of locating and repairing the leak itself. This distinction catches many homeowners off guard during a claim, so it's worth checking a specific policy's language before assuming either way.

Documenting the damage with photos before any cleanup begins, and keeping records of when symptoms first appeared, both strengthen a claim regardless of what the policy ultimately covers.

How Long Does Detection and Repair Take?

The detection process itself, acoustic listening and thermal imaging to narrow down the leak's location, typically takes a few hours for a residential property. The repair timeline afterward depends on the approach chosen: a direct spot repair through a limited access point is often completed the same day or the next, while rerouting a line above the slab can take somewhat longer depending on the home's layout.

Either way, the timeline is almost always shorter than homeowners initially expect once they learn the actual scope of work involved, which is usually far more contained than the whole-floor demolition many people picture when they first hear the words slab leak.

Preventing the Next One

There's no way to fully prevent a slab leak in a home with aging copper lines, since the underlying cause is gradual corrosion that happens regardless of how carefully a house is maintained. What does help is catching one early, before it's had months to cause secondary damage, and being aware that a single slab leak on an aging system sometimes signals more are likely to follow if the rest of the system is the same age and material.

A home that's had two or more slab leaks within a relatively short span is a reasonable candidate for a broader repiping conversation, since at that point the pattern points toward systemic corrosion rather than one isolated weak spot.

Keeping a simple log of any leak repairs, dates, locations, and what was found, makes that pattern much easier to recognize over time than relying on memory alone.

None of this means every homeowner needs to panic over a single leak. It's a matter of paying attention to the pattern over time rather than treating each incident as a completely isolated event.

Full List of Signs to Watch For

  • A warm or hot spot on an otherwise cool concrete floor
  • The sound of running water with every fixture off
  • A water bill that's risen with no change in household usage
  • Damp carpet or flooring with no visible source nearby
  • Low water pressure isolated to one part of the house

What Happens If You Wait

An unaddressed slab leak doesn't just waste water. Sustained moisture under a slab can eventually affect flooring, and in some cases the foundation itself, turning a targeted plumbing repair into a much larger and more expensive restoration project.

Catching the signs early keeps the fix contained to what it actually is: a small, locatable leak in a specific section of pipe.

If any of these signs sound familiar, a slab leak detection visit uses acoustic and thermal equipment to find the exact location before anything gets opened up, and where the leak has already caused visible flooring damage, restoration work addresses that alongside the repair. This comes up often enough in Scott's Mill, where homes from the late 1990s and early 2000s are reaching the age where copper hot water lines start showing wear.
