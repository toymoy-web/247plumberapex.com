PEX vs. Copper: What Wake County Homeowners Should Know About Repiping

By the Apex 24/7 Plumbing team · 2026-09-15

Have this exact problem right now? Skip the reading and call (919) 944-7281 directly.

Once a home reaches the point where a full repipe genuinely makes sense, usually after multiple pipe failures or clear signs of widespread corrosion, the next real question is what material to replace the old system with. For decades, copper was the default answer. Today, PEX has become the standard choice for most residential repipes, and it's worth understanding why.

What Actually Fails in Old Copper Systems

Copper is a genuinely durable material, but it corrodes internally over decades, especially in areas with certain water chemistry profiles. That corrosion is exactly what's usually driving a repiping conversation in the first place: pinhole leaks, discolored water, and pipes thinned enough that multiple failures start happening within a short span.

Why PEX Resists That Same Failure Mode

PEX is a flexible plastic tubing that doesn't corrode the way metal pipe does, which directly addresses the primary reason most repipes happen in the first place. It's also more resistant to the kind of freeze damage that cracks rigid copper pipe during an unexpected hard freeze, since PEX has some flexibility to expand slightly under pressure.

Installation Differences That Matter

PEX's flexibility also makes installation faster and often less invasive than copper, since it can be run through tighter spaces and around obstacles with fewer joints and fittings than rigid copper pipe requires. Fewer joints means fewer potential future leak points, which is a real, practical advantage beyond just installation speed.

For a whole-home repipe specifically, this often translates to less wall access needed and a shorter overall project timeline compared to an equivalent copper repipe.

Where Copper Still Makes Sense

Copper remains a valid choice for homeowners who specifically prefer it, whether for its long track record, resale considerations in certain markets, or personal preference. Some local codes or specific applications also still call for copper in particular situations, so it's not that copper has become obsolete, just that it's no longer the default recommendation it once was.

Cost Comparison

PEX generally costs less than copper for both material and labor, given faster installation and fewer required fittings. For a whole-home repipe, this difference adds up to a meaningful cost gap between the two options, on top of PEX's corrosion resistance advantage.

What About Taste or Water Quality Concerns?

Some homeowners ask whether PEX affects water taste compared to copper. Modern PEX products are certified for potable water use and don't meaningfully affect taste for the vast majority of households. Any taste or odor concern in Apex's water supply is far more likely related to municipal treatment chemistry than to the pipe material carrying it.

Making the Decision for Your Home

For most Wake County homes undergoing a repipe today, PEX is the practical, cost-effective recommendation given its corrosion resistance and installation advantages. The final decision still comes down to the specific home's situation and the homeowner's own priorities, which is worth discussing directly rather than assuming one material is automatically correct for every case.

If your home has had multiple pipe failures or shows signs of widespread corrosion, a repiping evaluation looks at the whole system and lays out real options, including material choice, before any work begins, and if a specific leak needs addressing first, leak detection locates it precisely. This conversation comes up regularly in older sections of Haddon Hall, where the earliest phases of the community are approaching 30 years old.

What a Repipe Project Timeline Looks Like

A whole-home repipe typically takes several days depending on the home's size and layout, with water service interrupted in stages rather than for the entire project. PEX's faster installation time compared to copper often shortens this timeline noticeably, which matters for households trying to minimize disruption to daily routines.

Drywall access and repair, where needed to reach interior walls, is usually the longer-lasting disruption rather than the pipe installation itself, regardless of which material is chosen.

A Practical Recommendation

For the overwhelming majority of Wake County homes undergoing a repipe today, PEX is the practical, cost-effective recommendation given its corrosion resistance and installation advantages. The final decision still comes down to the specific home's situation and the homeowner's own priorities, which is worth discussing directly rather than assuming one material is automatically correct for every case.

Does PEX Have Any Real Downsides?

PEX isn't without limitations. It shouldn't be used outdoors or anywhere exposed to direct UV light, since sunlight degrades the material over time. It also has a slightly lower maximum temperature tolerance than copper, which rarely matters in a typical residential application but is worth knowing for specialized uses like certain high-temperature heating systems.

For standard residential water supply lines inside a home, neither limitation is a practical concern, which is part of why PEX has become the default recommendation for most repiping projects.

Mixing Materials During a Partial Repipe

Not every repiping project replaces the entire system at once. Where only certain sections are being addressed, PEX can connect to existing copper using the correct transition fittings, allowing a targeted repipe of the worst sections without necessarily requiring a full whole-home replacement in every case.

This approach works well when a clear pattern points to specific problem areas rather than uniform corrosion throughout the entire system, which is exactly the kind of distinction a proper evaluation is meant to identify before any work begins.

Whichever scope makes sense for a specific home, the goal is the same: a system that stops producing new leaks year after year, not just a patch on the most recent one.

How to Prepare for a Repiping Evaluation

Before a repiping evaluation, it helps to have a rough timeline of past pipe failures, including approximate dates and locations if they're remembered, along with any prior repair invoices. This history gives a much clearer picture of the system's actual condition than a single snapshot inspection alone can provide.

It's also useful to flag any rooms or areas that have never had an issue, since that information helps distinguish between a system failing broadly versus a specific run of pipe that happens to be more exposed or more heavily used than the rest of the house, which changes the realistic scope of what actually needs replacing.

Whether the answer ends up being a full whole-home repipe or a more limited, targeted repair, walking in with that history ready leads to a faster, more accurate evaluation than starting from scratch on the day of the visit, and it often saves a follow-up appointment that would otherwise be needed just to gather the same information in person.

A repipe is a significant project, but for the right home it's the fix that actually resolves the underlying problem for good rather than one more repair added to a growing list, year after year, on the same aging system.
