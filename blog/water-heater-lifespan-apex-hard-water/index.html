How Long Should a Water Heater Last in Apex, NC's Hard Water?

By the Apex 24/7 Plumbing team · 2026-09-15

Have this exact problem right now? Skip the reading and call (919) 944-7281 directly.

A standard tank water heater is typically rated for 8 to 12 years of service. In Apex, NC, that number often lands closer to the lower end of that range, and the reason comes down to something most homeowners never think about: what's actually in the water flowing through the tank every day.

Municipal water through Town of Apex Water Resources runs on the harder side for the region. Hardness itself isn't a health concern, but the minerals responsible for it, mostly calcium and magnesium, settle out of water as it heats, and a water heater tank is exactly where that settling happens.

Where the Mineral Buildup Actually Goes

Every time water heats inside a tank, a small amount of dissolved mineral content drops out of solution and settles at the bottom. Over months and years, that sediment layer builds up thick enough to insulate the bottom of the tank from the heating element or burner beneath it.

That insulating layer forces the heater to work harder and longer to reach the same water temperature, which increases energy use and puts more stress on the heating element or burner assembly. In gas units, sediment can also cause a rumbling or popping sound as trapped water boils beneath the layer.

Signs Your Water Heater Is Aging Faster Than It Should

  • Rumbling, popping, or knocking sounds during heating
  • Longer wait times for hot water than when the unit was new
  • Rusty or discolored water, especially right after the tap is first turned on
  • A noticeable increase in energy bills without other explanation
  • The unit is 8 or more years old and has never been flushed

Does Flushing Actually Help?

Yes, meaningfully. An annual flush removes sediment before it builds up to a level that seriously affects performance. Homes in Apex specifically benefit more from this than homes in areas with naturally softer water, simply because there's more mineral content to deal with in the first place.

A flush won't undo years of accumulated buildup on a unit that's never had one, but starting the habit on a newer unit, or even catching up on an older one before symptoms get severe, extends realistic service life noticeably.

Gas vs. Electric: Does Fuel Type Change the Math?

Both gas and electric tank water heaters are affected by hard water sediment in roughly the same way, since the buildup happens regardless of what's heating the tank. The practical difference shows up in the symptoms: gas units tend to produce the rumbling or popping noise more noticeably, since sediment sits directly over the burner, while electric units are more likely to show gradually declining performance as the lower heating element becomes insulated by buildup.

A tankless unit sidesteps the sediment-in-a-tank problem entirely, since there's no standing water reservoir to accumulate buildup the same way. It still needs periodic descaling of its heat exchanger for the same underlying reason, mineral content in the water, but the maintenance pattern and failure mode are different enough that it's worth understanding separately.

What a Realistic Timeline Looks Like in Apex

A well-maintained tank unit with annual flushing can reasonably reach the upper end of its rated lifespan, 10 to 12 years, even with Apex's harder water. A unit that's never been flushed often starts showing real symptoms by year 6 or 7 and may need replacement well before the manufacturer's stated maximum, simply because sediment has been accumulating unchecked the entire time.

The gap between those two outcomes is almost entirely explained by maintenance, not by the unit's inherent quality or the household's water usage pattern. Two identical water heaters in similar Apex homes can have meaningfully different service lives based on whether one got flushed annually and the other never did.

Keeping a simple record of the unit's install date and flush history makes it much easier to have this conversation confidently when the time eventually comes, rather than guessing at the tank's age from memory alone.

What to Do Before You Call

  • Note when the symptoms started and how they've changed over time.
  • Check the unit's age from the installation date or manufacturer label if available.
  • Shut off the water heater's power or gas supply if it's actively leaking.

A Simple Way to Think About It

If a water heater is under 6 years old, a specific problem is almost always worth repairing rather than replacing outright. If it's over 8 or 9 years old and showing more than one symptom at once, sediment noise plus declining hot water output, for example, replacement usually makes more financial sense than chasing repairs on a unit that's likely near the end of its realistic service life regardless of what specifically fails next.

The one case where this rule of thumb doesn't apply is when the tank itself is actively leaking. A leaking tank body, as opposed to a leaking fitting or valve, means replacement regardless of the unit's age, since a tank leak isn't something that can be safely repaired.

Sizing Matters as Much as Age

A water heater that's constantly running out of hot water isn't necessarily failing. It might simply be undersized for the household's actual demand, especially if the home's occupancy has changed since the unit was installed, more people, more showers, a new appliance added to the mix. Replacing an undersized unit with one properly matched to current usage often solves a problem that looked like a maintenance issue but was really a sizing mismatch from the start.

This is worth raising during any repair conversation, since a technician diagnosing a specific symptom might not otherwise know the household's hot water demand has grown since the original installation.

When Repair Stops Making Sense

A unit under 6 years old with a specific, isolated problem, a failed thermostat or a fitting leak, is almost always worth repairing. A unit past 8 or 9 years old that's showing multiple symptoms at once, sediment noise, declining performance, and rising energy costs together, is usually a better candidate for replacement than another repair.

Whole-home water softening is worth considering for households planning to keep the same home long-term, since it addresses the root cause of accelerated wear across the water heater, fixtures, and appliances, not just the tank alone.

If your unit is showing several of these signs at once, a straight answer about repair versus replacement is worth getting before spending money on a fix that might not last. A water heater repair visit checks the tank directly rather than guessing from symptoms alone, and if replacement genuinely makes more sense, a water heater installation gets sized correctly for the household rather than just matching what's already there. Homes in Haddon Hall, where many units are original to homes built in the late 1990s, are a common example of exactly this decision point.
