Why Does My Apex Home Have Low Water Pressure?

By the Apex 24/7 Plumbing team · 2026-09-15

Have this exact problem right now? Skip the reading and call (919) 944-7281 directly.

Low water pressure is frustrating precisely because the cause isn't obvious from the symptom alone. A weak shower could mean something as simple as a clogged showerhead, or it could point to a much more serious issue with the main line feeding the house. The pattern of where the low pressure shows up is the biggest clue.

Low Pressure at One Fixture Only

If just one faucet or showerhead is weak while everything else in the house runs normally, the cause is almost always local to that fixture. A clogged aerator, mineral buildup inside a showerhead, or a partially closed shutoff valve under that specific sink are all common, inexpensive fixes.

This is a common, inexpensive fix in Apex's established neighborhoods like Beaver Creek and Haddon Hall, where original fixtures from the 1990s and 2000s have had the most time to accumulate mineral buildup.

Apex's harder municipal water makes mineral clogging inside fixture aerators and showerheads more common here than in areas with softer water, which is worth checking first before assuming anything more serious is wrong.

Low Pressure Throughout the Whole House

When every fixture in the house runs weak, the cause is upstream of the individual fixtures, somewhere in the main line, the pressure regulator, or occasionally a partially closed main shutoff valve that never got fully reopened after previous work.

A failing pressure-reducing valve, common on many residential systems, can also cause whole-house low pressure as it ages. This component regulates incoming municipal pressure down to a safer level for household plumbing, and when it fails, pressure throughout the house drops rather than spikes. Any valve replacement follows current Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requirements as adopted under North Carolina's plumbing code.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Is it one fixture or every fixture in the house?
  • Did the drop happen suddenly or build up gradually?
  • Has a neighbor mentioned the same issue recently?

Could It Be a Municipal Issue, Not Your Home?

Occasionally, low pressure isn't a home plumbing problem at all. Municipal water main work, a nearby fire hydrant in use, or high-demand periods across the utility's service area can temporarily reduce incoming pressure to an entire neighborhood. This usually resolves on its own and affects multiple homes at once, not just one.

If a neighbor mentions the same issue around the same time, that's a reasonable sign the cause is upstream of any individual home's plumbing, worth a quick check with Town of Apex Water Resources before assuming something inside the house has failed.

The Aerator Test Anyone Can Do

Before assuming a bigger problem, unscrew the aerator, the small mesh screen at the tip of a faucet, and run the water directly from the spout without it. If pressure improves noticeably, the aerator itself was clogged with sediment or mineral scale, an inexpensive, five-minute fix rather than a plumbing emergency.

The same logic applies to a showerhead: many models unscrew easily for a similar test, and a showerhead-specific descaling soak in vinegar often restores flow without any replacement needed at all.

What About Old Galvanized Pipe?

Homes in Apex's older, established neighborhoods sometimes still have original galvanized supply lines, and these pipes narrow internally over decades as corrosion builds up along the inside wall. This produces a gradual, whole-house pressure decline that's easy to attribute to the water utility or to simply assume is normal for an older home.

It isn't something to just live with. A pipe narrowed by internal corrosion is delivering less water than it was designed to, and depending on how advanced the corrosion is, that's either a sign a full evaluation is worth scheduling or, in more severe cases, an early indicator that a broader repiping conversation is overdue.

Pressure Problems After Recent Plumbing Work

If low pressure started right after a repair, a fixture installation, or any nearby construction, that timing itself is a useful clue. A newly installed shutoff valve that wasn't fully reopened, a fitting that's slightly misaligned, or debris introduced into the line during the work can all cause a sudden pressure change that traces directly back to that specific event rather than a separate, unrelated problem.

Mentioning any recent work when describing the issue helps narrow down the cause considerably faster than treating it as an unrelated mystery.

The Bigger Picture: Pressure Is a System, Not a Fixture

It's worth remembering that water pressure at any single tap is the end result of the entire system working together: the municipal supply, the meter, the main line, the pressure regulator if one's installed, and finally the branch line and fixture itself. A problem anywhere along that chain shows up as "low pressure" at the fixture, even though the fixture itself is often completely fine.

That's the real reason a systematic check, starting broad (whole house or one fixture) and narrowing down from there, beats guessing at any single cause first. It saves time and often avoids paying for a repair that wouldn't have actually fixed the problem.

When Low Pressure Actually Means High Pressure Elsewhere

Occasionally what feels like low pressure at one fixture is actually a sign that another part of the system is drawing too much water at the same time, running the dishwasher, washing machine, and a shower simultaneously, for example. This isn't a plumbing defect so much as a demand issue, and it's worth checking whether the low-pressure complaint only happens during these overlapping usage moments before assuming a system-wide problem.

If pressure stays consistently low even with no other fixtures in use, that rules out simple demand overlap and points back toward one of the causes covered above.

Whatever the cause turns out to be, describing exactly when and where the low pressure happens gives a plumber a real head start on diagnosing it correctly the first visit.

A Sudden Drop Is a Different Situation

Pressure that drops gradually over months points toward mineral buildup narrowing pipes internally. Pressure that drops suddenly, over hours or days, is a stronger signal of an active leak somewhere in the system, since water escaping the line reduces the pressure available at every fixture.

Quick Checklist Before Calling

  • Is it one fixture or the whole house?
  • Did it happen gradually or suddenly?
  • Has the water bill changed recently?
  • Is there any visible wet spot in the yard or under a sink?
  • Has any nearby construction or digging happened recently?

When It's Worth a Professional Look

A single clogged fixture is a reasonable DIY fix. Whole-house low pressure, a sudden drop, or any sign of a possible leak is worth a real diagnosis rather than guessing, since a genuine leak wastes water and money every day it goes unaddressed.

A leak detection visit uses acoustic and pressure-based equipment to check for a hidden leak if that's suspected, and if the issue traces back to the buried line itself, a water line repair addresses it at the actual source rather than treating a symptom. This is a common question in Salem, where quarter-acre lots mean longer buried supply runs than in denser, older parts of Apex.
