A slab leak happens when a water line running underneath a home's concrete foundation develops a leak, and it's one of the more disruptive-sounding plumbing problems precisely because people assume it means breaking up the entire floor. In most cases, once the leak is actually located, only a small section needs to be accessed.

In Apex, NC, slab leaks trace back most often to a hot water line whose copper has thinned from years of mineral-heavy water, or to shifting in Wake County's clay soil putting stress on a line that was never designed to flex.

Why Slab Leaks Happen in Apex

Copper hot water lines are more prone to slab leaks than cold lines, since hot water accelerates the corrosion process inside the pipe over years. Clay soil movement under the slab is the other major factor, since even small shifts in the ground beneath a foundation put uneven pressure on pipes that were installed straight and unstressed.

Poor initial installation, a line laid against sharp aggregate in the original concrete pour, can also create a wear point that eventually fails years later.

Signs of a Slab Leak

  • A warm or hot spot on an otherwise cool concrete floor
  • The sound of running water with every fixture off
  • A rising water bill with no other explanation
  • Damp carpet or flooring with no visible source
  • Low water pressure isolated to one part of the house

Finding It Without Breaking Up the Floor

Acoustic detection equipment listens for the specific sound of water escaping under pressure through a slab, and thermal imaging can confirm a hot water line leak by detecting the warm spot it creates on the floor above it. Together, these narrow the leak down to a small, specific area before any concrete is touched.

Once located, access is usually limited to that one section rather than the entire slab, and the repair itself, or in some cases rerouting the line above the slab instead of through it, gets decided based on what's actually most practical for that specific spot.

Repair Options Once Located

Small, accessible section

Direct spot repair through a limited access point in the slab.

Line difficult to access under slab

Rerouting the line above the slab or through a wall cavity instead.

Multiple leaks or heavily corroded line

Full repiping of that line, worth discussing as part of a broader repiping evaluation.

Cold water line affected

Same detection approach, less common but handled the same way.

Before Help Arrives

  • Avoid running hot water unnecessarily if a hot line slab leak is suspected.
  • Keep the warm or damp area clear so it can be inspected easily.
  • Photograph any visible flooring or carpet damage for insurance purposes.

Cost and Insurance

A targeted spot repair costs considerably less than the whole-floor demolition many homeowners initially fear, since detection equipment narrows the work area down before anything is opened up. Rerouting a line above the slab sometimes costs more upfront but avoids future slab access entirely.

Homeowners insurance often covers the resulting water damage from a slab leak but typically not the plumbing repair itself. Check the specific policy before assuming coverage either way. Any repair or reroute follows current Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requirements as adopted under North Carolina's plumbing code.
