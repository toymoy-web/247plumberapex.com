Whole-Home Repiping in Apex, NC

When a home has had multiple pipe failures over a short period, repiping addresses the underlying corrosion rather than fixing the same symptom repeatedly.

Repiping comes up when a home's plumbing system, usually original galvanized or aging copper, has reached the point where individual repairs are no longer solving the underlying problem. A single burst pipe is a repair. Two or three in different parts of the house within a year or two is a sign the whole system is corroding, not just the one section that happened to fail first.

In Apex, NC, this shows up most in established neighborhoods with homes built before the 1990s that have never had a full repipe, where galvanized supply lines have been quietly narrowing and weakening from the inside for decades.

A corroded original fitting alongside the new copper section that replaced it on a real Apex repipe job.

Signs a Full Repipe Makes Sense

Discolored water, especially a rusty or brownish tint that isn't tied to a specific recent event, points to widespread internal corrosion rather than a single failing section. Consistently low water pressure throughout the house, not isolated to one fixture, is another strong indicator, since a narrowing pipe interior restricts flow everywhere it occurs.

A pattern of repeated pinhole leaks or bursts in different locations over a relatively short time is the clearest sign, since it means the pipe material itself is failing broadly rather than one weak point having given out.

When to Seriously Consider Repiping

  • Multiple pipe failures in different locations within a year or two
  • Consistently discolored or rusty water throughout the house
  • Low water pressure at every fixture, not just one
  • Original galvanized supply lines in a home built before the 1990s
  • Visible corrosion on exposed sections of pipe

Evaluating the Full System

A repiping evaluation looks at the entire system, not just the fixture or section that prompted the call, checking pipe material, age, and condition throughout the accessible parts of the home to determine whether a full repipe or a more targeted set of repairs is the right call.

PEX has become the standard replacement material for most residential repipes given its resistance to corrosion and flexibility during installation, though copper remains an option where it's specifically preferred or required.

The evaluation also documents which sections of the system, if any, are still sound enough to leave in place, since a full repipe isn't always necessary even when the overall pattern points toward one.

Repipe Scope Options

Whole-house repipe

Full replacement of all supply lines, the most thorough and disruptive option.

Targeted section repipe

Replacing the most problematic runs while leaving sound sections in place.

Repeated spot repairs

Sometimes still reasonable for an otherwise sound system with one weak area.

New material selection

PEX for most residential applications, with copper where specifically preferred.

Third Leak This Year?

That's a system problem, not three separate coincidences.

What to Do Before an Evaluation

  • Note the location and approximate date of each past pipe failure to share with the technician.
  • Shut off water to any currently active leak if the valve is accessible.
  • Keep any prior repair records or invoices handy for context on what's already been done.

Cost and Timeline

A full repipe costs significantly more upfront than another round of spot repairs, but for a system failing broadly, it eliminates the recurring repair costs and water damage risk that come with waiting for the next failure. Cost depends on the home's size, wall accessibility, and whether drywall repair is included after pipe access.

Timeline for a whole-home repipe typically runs several days depending on the home's size and layout, with water service interrupted in stages rather than for the entire duration.
